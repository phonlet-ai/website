    +HOW IT WORKS/01

    From text to trigger. In seconds.

    Phonlet Wake listens for sounds, not for one memorised phrase. That is why a new wake word needs no new model.

    1. hey nova
      01 / TYPE

      Write the phrase.

      Any English words, names included. Unknown words get a predicted pronunciation.

    2. HHEYNOWVAH
      02 / PRONOUNCE

      Hear it as sounds.

      The phrase becomes phonemes, the units the model already knows.

    3. 10 / h0.1 / h
      03 / CALIBRATE

      Thresholds, calibrated.

      Two thresholds set from the phrase’s predicted false alarms. No tuning by hand.

    4. WAKE
      04 / LISTEN

      One model wakes.

      The same 1 MB model listens for every phrase, offline, on the device.

    +FOOTPRINT/02

    Small enough for a microcontroller.

    One int8 acoustic model of about 1 MB listens for every phrase, and a phrase is only data. The engine streams audio in 32 ms frames and keeps a small state between them, so the whole thing runs in about 1 MB of memory, on a laptop or on a microcontroller.

    +COMPARED/05

    openWakeWord, microWakeWord and Phonlet Wake.

    openWakeWord and microWakeWord are good open-source wake-word engines, and both train a small model for each word. Phonlet Wake skips the training run: one model hears the sounds of English, so a new phrase is only its pronunciations and two thresholds.

    +QUESTIONS/07

    Curious? Good.

    01How can a wake word work without recordings?

    The model hears the sounds of English speech, not one fixed phrase. A wake word is a list of pronunciations plus two calibrated thresholds, so adding one never changes the model.

    02What about words that are not in the dictionary?

    Names and made-up words get a pronunciation predicted from their spelling, marked “Predicted” in the lab. You can also write the sounds yourself, for example zorblax=Z AO R B L AE K S.

    03How accurate is it?

    On Picovoice’s public wake-word benchmark Phonlet Wake detects 94.9% of the test words in 10 dB noise at 0.1 false alarms per hour, with phrases typed as text, and 99.2% without noise. Every phrase is calibrated to a false-alarm target, from about 10 per hour of speech at full sensitivity to 0.1.

    04How is it different from openWakeWord and microWakeWord?

    Both train a small model for each wake word from synthetic speech: openWakeWord runs on Linux-class CPUs, microWakeWord on the ESP32-S3. Phonlet Wake needs no training run. One model hears the sounds of English, so a new phrase is ready in seconds, many phrases share one 1 MB model, and the same file runs from a browser to an ESP32-S3.

    05Does my audio leave the device?

    No. The engine runs where the microphone is. On this page it runs in a WebAssembly worker in your tab: nothing is uploaded, stored or recorded.

    06What makes a good wake word?

    Two to four syllables with sounds that are rare in everyday speech. “Hey barista” works well; “the” never will. The generator warns when a phrase is too short, too long or too common.
